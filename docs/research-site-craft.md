# Site craft — how the best film/tech sites of 2026 are actually built

Researched 2026-11-03 for `verse-site` (static, vanilla, obsidian/gold; pages index / verse / dailies / workflow / rights / verify / passport / console / tools).
Method: 20+ web queries against live 2026 pages, plus a read of our own build (`verse-site/*`, `runs/*/run.json`, `dailies.data.js`).
Constraint honoured throughout: **nothing here asks us to invent a metric, a number, or a showreel.** Where a technique needs data we do not have, it is marked `NEEDS DATA`.

## 1. Reference calibre: what the top tier actually does

The award tier in 2026 is not "more WebGL". A juror's own ranking of the year's best sites reduces it to three simultaneous conditions: art direction that survives as a *still frame*, motion that is *directed* (choreography with meaning, not effects), and performance that holds "around 60fps on mid-range mobile" and paints fast under throttling — and the reviewer's explicit test is to "kill the motion in your head", throttle the device, and confirm a `prefers-reduced-motion` fallback exists (https://www.hontran.dev/blog/best-award-winning-websites-2026).

Concrete, copyable behaviours observed:

- **A24 (real, official)** is a Next.js App-Router site whose hero is a *stacking carousel of static landscape stills*, not a video wall; trailers open through an in-house `VideoModalProvider` (self-hosted, no YouTube iframe); film stills render through a `DotcomImage` component carrying **focal points**; credits are structured data rendered via `LabeledItems` ("Written & Directed By" + name pairs); footer is nav + subscribe + legal (https://a24films.com/). The lesson: a real film company ships *stills first, video on intent*, and typesets credits as data, not as a picture.
- **Igloo Inc** (the reference WebGL site) renders its *UI text inside WebGL* with SDF texture offsets so glitch effects never retrigger DOM layout, compresses volumetric data through a custom VDB exporter, and grows ice crystals procedurally rather than shipping a mesh (https://webgpu.com/showcase/igloo-inc-procedural-crystals/; https://www.awwwards.com/igloo-inc-case-study.html).
- The cinematic-site canon for 2026 — Igloo, Active Theory, Lusion, Bruno Simon, Streets of Punk (pinned scroll-film), Locomotive (type-led smooth scroll), Obys (poster-scale serif), Apple (scroll-scrubbed frames), Basement Studio — shares one engineering rule: "runs on the GPU, the text stays real HTML" (https://dappasol.com/guides/cinematic-website-examples/; https://dappasol.com/guides/3d-website-examples/).
- Kinetic type done at award level is *legibility-first*: Mat Voyce's site is entirely type-in-motion, with timelines tuned so animation never blocks reading; Iventions uses Three.js for *lighting* ("atmosphere instead of spectacle"); Minh Pham layers a GSAP motion system over WebGL so the 3D frames the work rather than dominating it (https://www.hontran.dev/blog/best-award-winning-websites-2026).
- Tribute/concept tier: the A24-as-discs concept is WebGL + GSAP, "cinema you flip through" (https://www.raviklaassens.com/work/a24). The 2026 SOTD pattern for film-adjacent storytelling is a *pinned* scroll-film section (https://www.awwwards.com/inspiration/home-slider-siena-film-foundation, https://www.awwwards.com/inspiration/scroll-animation-fpso-floating-production-storage-and-offloading-hanwha-ocean).
- Model vendors (Runway, fal, Luma, Higgsfield) all present output as **per-clip cards carrying the model/camera-control name next to the result** — e.g. fal lists 1500+ models with a playground per model (https://fal.ai/, https://fal.ai/video), Higgsfield names the model and camera control per shot (https://higgsfield.ai/ai-video), Luma's Dream Machine is a grid of generations with the input visible (https://lumalabs.ai/app). That is exactly our "model-and-licence per shot" story, already a familiar visual grammar to this audience.
- Real-time film credibility reference points: Epic MetaHuman as a framework site (https://www.metahuman.com/), UE5 + MetaHuman + AI used to make a stylised short (https://www.unrealengine.com/spotlights/crafting-stylized-digital-humans-on-daughter-of-the-inner-stars-with-metahuman-and-ue5), and the "cinematic → interactive" switch of The Matrix Awakens (https://www.unrealengine.com/blog/introducing-the-matrix-awakens-an-unreal-engine-5-experience).
- Footer/credits discipline is a real, specable thing in film: a billing block has a *fixed order* (presentation credits, possessory credit, above-title cast, title, post-title cast, department heads, producers, writers, "Directed by"), contractual minimum sizes (DGA: director ≥15% of title size in paid ads, 35% for outdoor with other names; writers ≥ the larger of producer/director), and ultra-condensed faces (Univers 39, Bee, Saira Extra Condensed, Antonio, **Bebas Neue** — one we already ship) (https://endcredits.pro/billing-block/, https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/movie-poster-credits-template/).

Sound: 2026 practice is gesture-gated, short, rare, and *never* automatic — browsers block audio until the first gesture, and the author's etiquette is a visible mute toggle plus not fighting screen readers (https://www.mewastudio.com/en/blog/sound-design-interaction-signature; https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/Web_Audio_API/Best_practices). Loading choreography is legitimate "psychological time" management, but only when it reflects real work (https://www.awwwards.com/a-round-up-of-the-best-loading-animations-1.html).

## 2. Showreel delivery that opens instantly on a phone

**Codec reality, 2026 (measured from 7M+ device sessions):** AV1 decode is ~91% overall, but **Safari is the holdout — macOS ~27%, iOS ~33%**; HEVC dominates Apple; **AV1 + HEVC together cover 99.6%** of sessions; VP9 Profile 0 decode is 99.87%, above H.264 Baseline's 99.80% (https://webcodecsfundamentals.org/datasets/codec-analysis-2026/). Safari ≥17 needs a hardware decoder for AV1 (https://www.testmuai.com/learning-hub/av1-browser-support/, https://caniuse.com/av1).
**Shipping rule:** two `<source>` elements, no JS detection — AV1 WebM first, H.264 MP4 second (https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/better-web-video-with-av1-codec). Encode with `-an` for decorative loops: audio tracks are pure waste on a hero (https://web.dev/learn/performance/video-performance).

**Hero/loop handling:** `poster` is the LCP candidate; `preload="metadata"` for visible players, `preload="none"` for user-initiated ones, and `loading="lazy"` **only** for offscreen media — an above-the-fold `loading="lazy"` delays the thing you most want painted (https://developer.chrome.com/docs/modern-web-guidance/guides/performance/lazy-load-video-and-audio). An unloaded `<video>` defaults to **300×150 intrinsic size**, so explicit integer `width`/`height` (or `aspect-ratio`) is mandatory or you get both layout shift *and* an element that may never intersect the viewport; a `poster` combined with `loading="lazy"` makes browsers wait for visibility before fetching the poster too (https://engineering.squarespace.com/blog/2026/how-to-use-standard-html-video-and-audio-lazy-loading-on-the-web-today).

**HLS vs progressive:** for a static marketing site, progressive MP4/WebM of a ≤10s clip beats HLS — LL-HLS buys 3–6s latency only with server support and partial segments, and adds muxer/CDN complexity for a file that is one bitrate anyway (https://www.mux.com/articles/hls-vs-dash-what-s-the-difference-between-the-video-streaming-protocols, https://www.wowza.com/blog/hls-vs-dash). Use HLS/per-title ladders the day we host the actual film for streaming, not for a 1.4MB preview (https://www.mux.com/articles/adaptive-bitrate-streaming-guide).

**Scroll-scrubbing:** two real techniques, both used by 2026 award sites.
- *Image-sequence scrub* (the Apple method): production guidance says spritesheets fail past ~100 frames, raw `<canvas>` gives blank frames on slow networks, and the reliable pattern is **`img.src` swap with two quality tiers (low-res first, high-res swapped in) plus a nearest-frame fallback** so fast scrolling never shows nothing (https://everythingflow.agency/blog/three-approaches-scroll-image-sequence).
- *True video scrub* via WebCodecs `VideoFrame`/`FrameDecoder` for scroll-synchronised playback — real and well documented, but Safari/iOS support and complexity mean it should be an enhancement (https://lionkeng.medium.com/a-tutorial-webcodecs-video-scroll-synchronization-8b251e1a1708, https://github.com/diffusionstudio/webcodecs-sync, https://developer.chrome.com/docs/web-platform/best-practices/webcodecs).
Both of these can be driven by **frames we already extract** from our own masters.

**HDR:** browsers that play HDR today do their own HDR→SDR conversion — Firefox on Windows uses BT.2020 with PQ and HLG and applies **Reinhard tone mapping at a fixed 400→100 cd/m²** in custom shaders rather than trusting the hardware video processor; macOS HDR is live, Windows is the shipping target, Linux/Android still thin (https://mozillagfx.wordpress.com/2026-07-14/hdr-video-in-firefox-for-windows-tech-retrospective/). There is **no standardised web tone-mapping model yet** (https://github.com/w3c-cg/ColorWeb-CG/issues/113; https://svgees.us/Talks/HDRExperts2025/W3C-HDR-20250302.pdf). Practical consequence for us: do the tone map ourselves in the mastering pass (ITU-R BT.2446-1 is the documented method for HDR→SDR conversion, https://www.itu.int/dms_pub/itu-r/opb/rep/R-REP-BT.2446-1-2021-PDF-E.pdf), ship BT.709/sRGB to the browser, and keep the HDR master only as a downloadable file whose colour space is stated in words — never claim "HDR in your browser" for a web play.

**Autoplay with sound:** effectively not available. Muted autoplay is always allowed; sound requires user interaction with the domain, a high Media Engagement Index (desktop) or an installed PWA/home-screen entry, and a blocked `play()` shows up as a rejected promise with `NotAllowedError` — which is the only honest way to detect it (https://developer.chrome.com/blog/autoplay). So: hero loops are silent; sound is a button that *starts after a click*, and the button's state persists (we already have the MOTION toggle; the same contract applies to SOUND).

## 3. 3D on a marketing site in 2026 without wrecking it

- **Library choice:** three.js is the pick for brand/marketing sites — ~185 kB gzip core (~300 kB `three/webgpu`), versus ~615 kB PlayCanvas and ~1.8 MB for all of `@babylonjs/core`; three's WebGPU is full since r171 and it wins on ecosystem and R3F integration (https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-vs-babylonjs-vs-playcanvas-comparison). Our `verse3d.min.js` is 506 kB (three + composer + bloom) and is currently *statically* imported by the homepage — the single biggest avoidable cost we have.
- **Real budgets:** target ~100 draw calls/frame on mobile (a few hundred on desktop), stay under ~100k vertices, cap device pixel ratio, ≤3 dynamic lights, shadow maps 512–1024 on mobile, KTX2/Basis textures use **4–8× less GPU memory** (a 4096² RGBA texture is 64 MiB VRAM, ~85 MiB with mipmaps), Draco/meshopt cut geometry ~87–95% (https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-best-practices-100-tips).
- **WebGPU** is now ~86–89% covered globally (all major engines shipping, macOS Tahoe 26 on ARM64 as of Firefox 145; Linux/Android/Intel-Mac still landing) — so it is a *bonus path*, not a gate (https://caniuse.com/webgpu, https://web.dev/blog/webgpu-supported-major-browsers, https://webgpu.com/news/webgpu-hits-critical-mass-all-major-browsers/).
- **Splats are a bandwidth/VRAM decision, not a shader decision.** 4M splats: raw PLY **1.4 GB**, SuperSplat-packed PLY 200–400 MB, **SOG 80–200 MB, SPZ 100–250 MB**, KSplat L1 150–300 MB; SPZ is recommended for smallest payload/fastest first paint on mobile (https://swyvl.io/blog/gaussian-splat-formats-ply-spz-ksplat/; uncompressed reference: ~220 MB per 1M splats, https://note.com/izutsuya/n/n45650a5fb0f4). PlayCanvas/SuperSplat guidance: splatting is *fill-rate* bound with heavy overdraw; default budget **1 million splats**, use `splatBudget`, streamed SOG for large scenes, disable anti-alias to cut fragment load (https://developer.playcanvas.com/user-manual/gaussian-splatting/building/performance/; https://www.cgchannel.com/2026/09/playcanvas-releases-supersplat-editor-3-0/). A 1–4M-splat scene is therefore a 100 MB–1.4 GB object: fine for an installed app, wrong for a phone on mobile data.
- **GLTF:** with Draco/meshopt + KTX2, a believable single-shot set build is 2–8 MB and that *is* a defensible page addition; a splat cloud is not.
- **Degrade honestly:** probe WebGL2 before importing three (`canvas.getContext('webgl2')`), respect `Save-Data`, `navigator.connection.effectiveType`, and coarse-pointer/small-viewport, and treat failure as normal — our own `try/catch` around `createVerse` already proves the pattern works; what it lacks is the *gate before download* (dynamic `import()`). Also handle `webglcontextlost` (GPU memory exhaustion is a common cause) rather than leaving a black canvas (https://svilenkovic.com/3d/webgl-context-lost-fix; https://stackoverflow.com/questions/9899807/three-js-detect-webgl-support-and-fallback-to-regular-canvas).
- **Metric targets stay the same for 3D pages:** LCP ≤2.5s, INP ≤200ms, CLS ≤0.1 at the 75th percentile (https://web.dev/articles/vitals) — and for the record, the *median* page in 2024–25 was 2,311–2,652 KB mobile/desktop, ~2,362 KB by July 2025 (https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/page-weight, https://css-tricks.com/http-archive-2025-web-almanac/), so a video-and-3D site that keeps its first 1.5 MB honest is already faster than the average brochure site.

## 4. Motion and accessibility, as a contract

- **`prefers-reduced-motion` is "remove", not "speed up"**: keep opacity/colour/instant state changes, drop translation, scaling, parallax and loops; treat it as an architecture decision, documented as an ADR (https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/Reference/At-rules/@media/prefers-reduced-motion, https://github.com/BjornMelin/dev-skills/blob/main/plugins/web-motion/skills/web-css-animations/references/docs-mdn-prefers-reduced-motion.md, https://architecture.lullabot.com/adr/20260522-reduced-motion-handling/, https://agnikii.co.uk/inclusive-design/prefers-reduced-motion/). Our CSS covers `.rise`, `.reel`, `.grade::after`; our JS covers the canvas. The gap is *scroll-linked video scrub* and the curtain, which must resolve to their end state instantly.
- **Native scroll timelines** are now the default tool: `animation-timeline: scroll()/view()` reports **88.25% global support**, shipped in Chrome, Edge, Firefox, Safari, Opera and Samsung Internet (https://caniuse.com/mdn-css_properties_animation-timeline_scroll). That replaces ScrollTrigger+GSAP for progress-driven fades and gives a compositor-driven, jank-free reveal at ~zero JS (https://modern-css.com/scroll-linked-animations-without-a-library/, https://www.joshwcomeau.com/animation/scroll-driven-animations/, https://vault.hyperiux.com/blog/native-scroll-timelines-are-here-does-every-scroll-effect-still-need-javascript). We already `@supports` it in one place — extend it, and keep GSAP only for SplitText-scale choreography.
- **Contrast:** AA is 4.5:1 normal text, 3:1 large text and non-text UI (https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html, https://web-accessibility-checker.com/en/blog/color-contrast-checker-wcag-guide) and low-contrast text is the single most common failure on the web — 83.9% of home pages (https://www.a11yflow.dev/blog/color-contrast-wcag-developer-guide). Dark cinematic UIs fail this exactly where gold label text on obsidian at 10–11 px fails; our own comment in `verse.css` measures `--gold-lbl` at 4.26:1, i.e. **below the floor we set for ourselves**. APCA previews are the right upgrade path but WCAG 2.x numbers are what a checker enforces (https://www.designsystemscollective.com/the-practical-guide-to-wcag-contrast-updated-for-wcag-2-2-apca-preview-19f1a4ca6be4).
- **Focus order:** never `outline: none` without an equal-contrast replacement; give a visible `:focus-visible` ring at ≥3:1 against its background and keep DOM order = visual order for pinned/overlaid sections.
- **Captions:** WCAG requires captions for prerecorded video (AA 1.2.2, with 1.2.5 audio description at AAA), WebVTT is the web format, sidecar closed captions are preferred over open/burned-in because **burned-in lines cannot be read by a screen reader, cannot be restyled, cannot be translated, and fail users who turn captions off** (https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/, https://aaardvarkaccessibility.com/wcag-plain-english/1-2-2-captions-prerecorded/, https://kreateflo.com/blog/what-are-the-rules-for-accessible-subtitles-under-wcag-standards, https://ollang.com/partners/subtitles-captions-that-work-specs-rates-accessibility). Burned-in subtitles stay in the *master* (festival delivery); the *web* player carries the `.vtt` track. Our dailies page already explains this distinction — make it the actual behaviour everywhere.
- **Screen readers on image-heavy dailies:** every stage frame needs a *content-bearing* alt ("shot01 after the motion pass — 119 frames, mean luma 58.6") not "image", purely decorative grain/vignette/canvas must stay `aria-hidden="true"` (already done), before/after compare images belong in a `<figure>` with a `<figcaption>` carrying the numbers, and JS-rendered tables get real `<table>`/`<th scope>` semantics plus a live region when the run selection changes.
- **Font loading:** preloading the display face (we preload Bebas Neue) is correct, but CLS-safe text requires a tuned fallback with `size-adjust` / `ascent-override` / `descent-override` so the Bebas swap does not shove the masthead (https://www.debugbear.com/blog/web-font-layout-shift, https://vincent.bernat.ch/en/blog/2024-cls-webfonts, https://web.dev/articles/optimize-cls).
- **Fluid type:** hero display type on 2026 marketing sites is `clamp()`-based and intentional from 360 px to 2560 px, with a *systematic* type+space scale rather than one big heading (https://jadapps.app/font-tools/guides/fluid-typography-real-world-examples, https://cssawwwards.com/blog/fluid-typography-css-clamp-guide-2026, https://madegooddesigns.com/web-typography-trends-2026/, https://thecrit.co/resources/css-typography-best-practices).

## 5. Trust machinery that already exists and can be shown honestly

See section (D) below for the exact names, versions, tools and URLs, plus what each one can and cannot prove. The single most important framing fact for a film platform: **detectors are weak and provenance is not truth.** Consumer-grade AI detectors run 70–90% true-positive with 5–15% false positives on human content, platform classifiers 80–95%, **video detection only 50–70%**, audio 90–98% (https://www.auditsocials.com/blog/ai-content-detection-technology-c2pa-watermarking-metadata-2026) — and the research community's answer is cross-layer auditing that flags *contradictions* between C2PA metadata and watermark signals (https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02378v1). A site that shows "what we signed, what we measured, and what we cannot claim" is doing something almost nobody else does.

## 6. Dailies: what a studio actually shows, and what a viewer understands

Real dailies tooling shows, per clip: camera/reel/scene/take identifiers, **checksum-verified** offload, LUT/colour application with a match wizard, audio waveform with sync offsets, error/health checks, and generated reports; on a camera-to-cloud set the script supervisor's **circle takes** and performance notes are what make a take mean something (https://pomfort.com/silverstackxt/features/, https://jonnyelwyn.co.uk/film-and-video-editing/creating-colour-graded-dailies-on-set-with-silverstack-lab/, https://blog.frame.io/2021-02-11/frameio-c2c-technical-guide/, https://blog.frame.io/2022-03-14/managing-tiered-dailies-workflow-frame-io-c2c/). The daily *progress* report is a different animal and is about schedule: pages, set-ups, scene count, **running time**, and the day's times — the numbers a producer reads (https://sethero.com/blog/script-supervisor-report-explained/, https://www.studiobinder.com/blog/script-supervisor-forms-template/).
What a non-industry viewer understands from that vocabulary: *which take, how long it took, whether a human had to finish it, whether the pixels changed, and how big the file is.* Everything else (codec strings, kbps, hashes) belongs in an expandable "the machine read" layer — which is precisely our brand.

# (A) Steal list — 20 techniques, each with source and reason

1. **Silent hero clip, `poster` as LCP, `preload="metadata"`, `-an` audio stripped.** https://web.dev/learn/performance/video-performance — a poster paints in one request while the clip arrives behind it; audio bytes on a looping hero are pure loss.
2. **Two `<source>` AV1→H.264 fallback with no browser sniffing.** https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/better-web-video-with-av1-codec + https://webcodecsfundamentals.org/datasets/codec-analysis-2026/ — AV1+HEVC reaches 99.6% of decode sessions but Safari desktop is only ~27%, so the second source is mandatory, not optional.
3. **Explicit integer `width`/`height` (or `aspect-ratio`) on every `<video>`; `loading="lazy"` only offscreen.** https://developer.chrome.com/docs/modern-web-guidance/guides/performance/lazy-load-video-and-audio — unloaded video is 300×150 by default: unsized media causes CLS *and* can never trigger its own lazy load.
4. **Image-sequence scroll scrub with two quality tiers and a nearest-frame fallback.** https://everythingflow.agency/blog/three-approaches-scroll-image-sequence — the Apple-grade effect with zero blank frames, and it runs on frames our ffmpeg pass already produces.
5. **WebCodecs `FrameDecoder` scroll-sync only as an enhancement.** https://lionkeng.medium.com/a-tutorial-webcodecs-video-scroll-synchronization-8b251e1a1708 — real frame-accurate video scrub where supported; the image tier keeps it honest everywhere else.
6. **CSS scroll-driven animations instead of a scroll library.** https://caniuse.com/mdn-css_properties_animation-timeline_scroll — 88.25% support today: progress-linked reveals in the compositor, no GSAP bytes, no main-thread scroll handler.
7. **Text stays real HTML; only the atmosphere goes to the GPU.** https://dappasol.com/guides/cinematic-website-examples/ — the single rule that separates an award site from an inaccessible canvas.
8. **WebGL-rendered UI text with SDF offsets when type itself is animated.** https://webgpu.com/showcase/igloo-inc-procedural-crystals/ — glitch/distort the headline without a single DOM relayout (use sparingly; keep the accessible copy in the tree).
9. **Pinned scroll-film section (one act = one pinned viewport).** https://www.awwwards.com/inspiration/home-slider-siena-film-foundation — the standard 2026 way to make a page read like an edit rather than a brochure.
10. **Kinetic type with a readability floor: timelines tuned so motion never blocks reading.** https://www.hontran.dev/blog/best-award-winning-websites-2026 (Mat Voyce) — the difference between premium lettering and motion noise.
11. **3D as lighting, not spectacle.** https://www.hontran.dev/blog/best-award-winning-websites-2026 (Iventions) — for a house whose hero is *film*, gold bloom as atmosphere beats a demo-reel of polygons.
12. **Juror's own acceptance test: static frame, throttled device, reduced-motion check.** https://www.hontran.dev/blog/best-award-winning-websites-2026 — three commands we can run on our own pages every build.
13. **Progressive load gate for 3D: dynamic `import()` behind WebGL2 probe + `Save-Data` + `effectiveType` + viewport.** https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-best-practices-100-tips — 506 kB of three.js must not be a homepage tax for a phone on 3G.
14. **KTX2/Basis + Draco/meshopt and a hard draw-call budget (~100 mobile, <100k verts, ≤3 lights).** https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-best-practices-100-tips — 4–8× less texture VRAM and ~87–95% smaller geometry is where 60 fps on a mid phone comes from.
15. **Splats only as SPZ/SOG, capped splat budget, streamed, with a stated VRAM/byte cost.** https://swyvl.io/blog/gaussian-splat-formats-ply-spz-ksplat/ + https://developer.playcanvas.com/user-manual/gaussian-splatting/building/performance/ — 4M splats is 1.4 GB raw vs 80–250 MB compressed, and the render is fill-rate bound: this is a byte decision before a visual one.
16. **Self-hosted progressive clips for marketing, HLS only when we host the film.** https://www.mux.com/articles/hls-vs-dash-what-s-the-difference-between-the-video-streaming-protocols — a 1.4 MB preview does not need a manifest, a segmenter or a CDN rule.
17. **Do our own HDR→SDR tone map in mastering (BT.2446-1), ship BT.709, and say so.** https://www.itu.int/dms_pub/itu-r/opb/rep/R-REP-BT.2446-1-2021-PDF-E.pdf + https://mozillagfx.wordpress.com/2026-07-14/hdr-video-in-firefox-for-windows-tech-retrospective/ — browsers tone map inconsistently and there is no standard model yet, so a web-grade SDR master is the honest deliverable.
18. **Gesture-gated SOUND button with persisted state, mirroring the MOTION toggle.** https://www.mewastudio.com/en/blog/sound-design-interaction-signature + https://developer.chrome.com/blog/autoplay — unmuted autoplay is blocked except by MEI/permission, so sound must be an offered affordance, never an ambush.
19. **Billing-block footer rendered from data, in condensed faces, in contractual order.** https://endcredits.pro/billing-block/ + https://a24films.com/ (`LabeledItems`) — a film house is read as amateur by its credit typesetting; ours can carry the real per-beat tool credit list straight from the run log.
20. **Sidecar WebVTT on the web player, burned-in only in the master, with the distinction shown as UI.** https://www.w3.org/WAI/media/av/captions/ + https://kreateflo.com/blog/what-are-the-rules-for-accessible-subtitles-under-wcag-standards — closed captions are machine-readable, restyleable and translateable; burned lines are none of those.
21. *(bonus)* **Stills-first film pages with focal-point-cropped images and video on intent.** https://a24films.com/ (`DotcomImage`, `VideoModalProvider`) — the actual A24 pattern: cheap, fast, indexable, and it puts the *frame* before the *player*.
22. *(bonus)* **Model/version named on the clip card.** https://higgsfield.ai/ai-video, https://fal.ai/ — the industry has already taught this audience to expect "which model made this", so our `used` + `tried[]` fields are a native grammar, not a new idea to explain.

# (B) Performance budgets (numbers to hold)

First byte / metrics at the 75th percentile (https://web.dev/articles/vitals):
- TTFB ≤ 800 ms · LCP ≤ 2.5 s (target ≤ 1.8 s mobile) · INP ≤ 200 ms (target ≤ 100 ms) · CLS ≤ 0.1 (target ≤ 0.02) · FCP ≤ 1.8 s.
- Reference point: the median page is ~2.3–2.9 MB (https://almanac.httparchive.org/en/2024/page-weight, https://css-tricks.com/http-archive-2025-web-almanac/) — a cinematic site is allowed to be heavier than median but should not be heavier than **3× median**.

Page weight ceilings for this site:
- **Critical path (everything needed for first paint): ≤ 150 KB HTML+CSS, ≤ 60 KB first-party JS, ≤ 25 KB fonts (subsetted woff2, 2 faces max), ≤ 240 KB hero still/poster.** Total ≤ 900 KB mobile / 1.2 MB desktop.
- **Homepage with all enhancements, 3D on: ≤ 4 MB total, of which ≤ 1.5 MB video.**
- **Any single progressive clip: ≤ 2.5 MB.** Preview at 720p/10s: **≤ 1.0 MB AV1 + ≤ 1.5 MB H.264.** Master 1080p stays off the page (download link with byte count stated).
- Encode targets: AV1 WebM `qp 30` (or `crf 32`), `-preset cpu`, GOP ≤ 2 s, `+faststart`; H.264 `crf 26–28` baseline/main profile, `-an` for loops (https://evilmartians.com/chronicles/better-web-video-with-av1-codec). Bitrate sanity: 720p loop ≈ 800–1200 kbps; 1080p review copy ≈ 3–5 Mbps (against suggested delivery 8–12 Mbps for 1080p streaming — a marketing loop should be a quarter of that: https://www.moonb.io/tools/video-bitrate-calculator).
- Poster/frames: AVIF ≤ 90 KB (1280 w) + JPEG fallback ≤ 150 KB, intrinsic dims exactly matching the video box (our current frames run 71–158 KB JPEG — already in range; publish a WebP/AVIF tier beside them).
- Image gallery (dailies): ≤ 16 frames on first paint, everything else `loading="lazy" decoding="async"`, all `<img>` dimensioned; total image bytes ≤ 1.2 MB.

3D/JS budgets:
- three.js core gzip ≈ 185 kB (https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-vs-babylonjs-vs-playcanvas-comparison); our bundled `verse3d.min.js` is 506 kB raw — after gzip roughly 140–160 kB, which is affordable **only if loaded behind a gate** (WebGL2 present, `Save-Data` absent, `effectiveType` ∉ {2g, slow-3g}, viewport ≥ 700 px, motion ON, and after first paint via `requestIdleCallback` + dynamic import).
- Draw calls ≤ 100/frame mobile (few hundred desktop); vertices ≤ 100 k; dynamic lights ≤ 3; shadow maps 512–1024 mobile; devicePixelRatio cap 1.75 desktop / 1.5 low-end (we already cap at 1.75); GPU texture memory ≤ 150 MB (a 4096² RGBA texture alone is 64 MiB → 85 MiB with mipmaps; KTX2 gives 4–8× relief) (https://www.utsubo.com/blog/threejs-best-practices-100-tips).
- Frame cost: 60 fps desktop, 30 fps floor mobile; pause on `visibilitychange` (done) **and** pause when the canvas leaves the viewport.
- Splats, if ever: SPZ or SOG only, ≤ 500 k splats on mobile with `splatBudget`, streamed LOD, and the byte/VRAM cost printed on the page. 4M-splat scene = 1.4 GB PLY / 80–250 MB SOG-SPZ (https://swyvl.io/blog/gaussian-splat-formats-ply-spz-ksplat/).
- Grain/vignette overlay: one 160 px SVG noise tile, static; animate with `transform` on the *overlay only*, never on `.reel` (transforming the content stream re-rasterises all text and is the opposite of a compositing cheap trick); disable entirely under `prefers-contrast: more` (done) and reduced motion.
- Data: `data.generated.min.js` is 281 kB and is parsed on every page for a sidebar that needs a dozen numbers. Budget: ≤ 20 kB of "shell facts" per page + the heavy catalogue fetched only by the pages that render it (tools/verse/search).

# (C) What we can build today with zero fake data

Every item below is driven only by files we already produce: `runs/<ts>/run.json`, per-run artifacts + ffprobe output, `verse-site/dailies.data.js`, extracted stage frames, `dialogue.vtt`, `status.json`. Nothing needs a number we do not have. Anything marked `NEEDS DATA` names the one log line we would have to start recording ourselves.

1. **An honest curtain.** Replace `step(12)` / `step(58)` / `step(100)` with counts of real awaited work — "3 of 11 frames decoded", "type loaded", "catalogue parsed" — using `document.fonts.ready`, `Image.decode()` and module import completion, with a 900 ms hard lift (the lift rule we already have). Zero invention, and it removes the only piece of theatre on the site.
2. **Where the minutes went.** A horizontal timing strip from `rows[].seconds` against `wallSeconds` — our 2026-11-02 run is 2.2 + 2.0 + 6.4 + 6.0 + 11.2 + **81.8** s, i.e. the finishing pass is 75% of the wall clock. This is a real, cinematic "one beat dominates the day" chart, and it explains itself.
3. **The refusal ledger.** `rows[].state == "failed"` + `memory[]` verbatim, rendered as cards with the licence-unread message, a count per tool, and the beat it blocked. Nothing on the web shows an AI film pipeline's refusals; ours are already in the log.
4. **The fallback ladder, per beat.** `tried[]` vs `used` as a chip sequence — `replicate ✗ → comfyui ✗ → blender ✗ → ffmpeg ✓` — with the refusal note as the tooltip. It is the most persuasive "we are not a wrapper" artefact we can make from `run.json`.
5. **Pixels-changed proof.** Per stage, a sparkline of `stats.yavg` / `stats.satavg`, and the existing `compare.dLuma` / `dSat` numbers between the subtitle-pass and the finish-pass, printed as "−6.51 luma, −9.64 saturation at t=8.2 s". This *is* the grade moving; it is measured, not asserted.
6. **Byte-per-second honesty.** From `artifact.bytes` and `probe` duration we already have kbps per stage; show "the finish master left the house at 49,628 kbit/s and the web preview is 1.44 MB — here is the 40× we made for your phone". It ships the delivery-craft story with our own numbers.
7. **Contact sheet of the rail.** One real frame per produced beat with `stage/scene`, `used`, `seconds`, `frame.bytes` and the exact `frame.at` timecode as its caption; failed beats appear as named gaps, not missing tiles. (Screen-reader-safe captions double as the alt text.)
8. **Plate → shot → master, scrubbed.** The Apple-style scrub built from *frames we extracted at real timecodes* from `film01-motion-shot01.mp4` etc., with the nearest-frame fallback; caption each stop with its actual `t=` value. No invented camera move — the scroll position maps to the shot's own timeline.
9. **A run-to-run spine.** `runs/*/` are timestamped and each has a `run.json`: plot `wallSeconds`, produced-vs-refused counts and per-beat state across every run we have done. A trendline of our own failures is the most honest marketing on the internet, and it cannot be faked because it is a directory listing.
10. **Fingerprint panel that verifies itself.** `status.json` already lists per-file sha256 and bytes: render it, and add a "check this page" control that hashes the file it is looking at and prints match/mismatch. Live build stamp (we show it) plus verifiable bytes is a trust feature nobody else on a film site has.
11. **Two caption tracks, one player.** WebVTT from `dialogue.vtt` as a real `<track kind="captions">` alongside the burned-in band image we already generate, with the explanation of why each exists — plus, `NEEDS DATA`: one extra ffmpeg pass (`-af ebur128`) per master to print measured integrated loudness in LUFS instead of any claim about "cinema levels".
12. **Cost line that says what it means.** `spentUsd: 0` printed as "0 USD for this run, because every beat that succeeded ran locally on ffmpeg; a paid model would appear here with its real number" — and, `NEEDS DATA`, a per-invocation spend field in the log so the day we use a paid model the number is real rather than estimated.

# (D) Trust machinery — exact standard names, tools, URLs, and what each can prove



**Provenance and signing**
- **C2PA (Coalition for Content Provenance and Authenticity)** — spec currently at **2.5 (October 2026)**, versions 1.0 (Dec 2021) → 2.5; manifest = **assertions**, **claims**, **signatures**; the spec states the basis of trust is "the identity of the signer" and requires **RFC 3161** timestamping. https://spec.c2pa.org/specifications/specifications/2.5/specs/C2PA_Specification.html
- **CAI (Content Authenticity Initiative)** open-source SDK — read/validate credentials, create manifests, sign and attach to supported media. https://opensource.contentauthenticity.org/ · https://contentcredentials.org/ · consumer verifier **Content Credentials Verify**: https://contentcredentials.org/verify and https://verify.contentauthenticity.org/ · third-party inspector https://c2paviewer.com/
- **Official implementations** (names matter for a credibility page): `c2pa-rs` (Rust, reference) https://github.com/contentauth/c2pa-rs, `c2pa-python`, `c2pa-java`, `c2pa-c`, `c2pa-node-v2`, `c2pa-ios`, and the **C2PA CLI** — all listed at https://c2pa.wiki/tools/official/
- **Watermarking: SynthID** (Google DeepMind) — imperceptible marks on AI images/audio/video/text, with a public **SynthID Detector** expanded in October 2026. https://deepmind.google/models/synthid/ · https://synthid.com/ · https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/google-deepmind/synth-id-ai-content/ · https://www.infoq.com/news/2026/10/synthid-watermarking-detector/
- **Cross-layer auditing** is the 2026 research position: verify C2PA *and* watermark signals together and flag contradictions. https://arxiv.org/html/2603.02378v1
- **Detection accuracy ceilings we should quote instead of our own opinions:** consumer detectors 70–90% TP / 5–15% FP on human content, platform classifiers 80–95%, **video 50–70%**, audio 90–98%; metadata forensics is the weakest layer (trivially stripped or forged). https://www.auditsocials.com/blog/ai-content-detection-technology-c2pa-watermarking-metadata-2026
- **Honest limits, in the standard's own terms:** credentials are tamper-evident statements, **not proof of truth**; manifests are stripped by re-encoding and screenshots/recaptures create unlinked files, so *absence of a credential is not evidence of AI*. https://www.textsight.ai/blog/c2pa-content-credentials-guide/

**Descriptor / vocabulary standards (the fields we fill per shot)**
- **IPTC Photo Metadata Standard 2025.1** (ratified November 2025; IPTC Extension schema revised to 1.9) — adds the four AI properties **AI Prompt Information**, **AI Prompt Writer Name**, **AI System Used**, **AI System Version Used**. http://www.iptc.org/std/photometadata/specification/IPTC-PhotoMetadata-2025.1.html · https://www.numonic.ai/blog/iptc-2025-c2pa-ai-provenance-metadata
- **IPTC `DigitalSourceType` controlled vocabulary** — `trainedAlgorithmicMedia` (fully generated) / `compositeWithTrainedAlgorithmicMedia` (mixed), the namespace `http://cv.iptc.org/newscodes/digitalsourcetype/`, already readable by mainstream tools. https://exifinjector.com/blog/label-ai-generated-images-with-metadata · https://community.adobe.com/questions-32/tagging-ai-images-in-the-image-metadata-xmp-etc-1643164
- **IPTC media-provenance implementation guidance for publishers** (where in the pipeline to apply C2PA + metadata, CMS screens, `c2pa-rs` libraries). https://iptc.org/media-provenance/how-do-i-implement/
- Note for our own copy: "IPTC" is a standards body, not a signature — the honest one-liner is *"each shot carries an IPTC 2025.1 `aiSystemUsed` / `aiSystemVersionUsed` descriptor and a C2PA 2.5 manifest"*.

**Law and policy that gives us language (all real, all 2026)**
- **EU AI Act, Article 50** — providers of AI generating synthetic audio/image/video/text must mark outputs **"in a machine-readable format and detectable as artificially generated or manipulated"**; deployers of deepfake-generating systems **shall disclose** the content is artificial. **Applies from 2 August 2026.** https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/50/
- **California AI Transparency Act, SB 942** (with **AB 853** amendments signed 13 Oct 2025) — in force **1 January 2026**; distinguishes **latent (embedded, machine-readable) disclosure** from **manifest (visible label/watermark) disclosure**; the *visible* watermark mandate was later removed, leaving latent provenance; applies at **1M monthly users** for covered providers and **2M** for large online platforms, and requires platforms to offer a UI letting consumers **inspect provenance data**. https://legiscan.com/CA/text/SB942/id/3013546 · https://www.troutmanprivacy.com/2025/10/california-ai-transparency-act-amendments-signed-into-law/ · https://www.lexology.com/library/detail.aspx?g=d77992d2-238a-4f2a-9b4d-42ef92ad5de9
- **YouTube altered/synthetic content disclosure** — creator self-declaration for photorealistic synthetic content, with platform-applied labels from **27 May 2026**; script-writing and caption generation are exempt. https://blog.youtube/news-and-events/disclosing-ai-generated-content/ · https://support.google.com/youtube/thread/424874071/updates-to-ai-content-disclosure-and-labels · https://air.io/en/youtube-hacks/youtube-ai-disclosure-in-2026-label-it-yourself-or-youtube-will
- **SAG-AFTRA AI terms** — informed consent required to create a **digital replica** of a performer's body/voice, separate compensation, and (2026 enforcement phase) restrictions on replicas of performers in other works without consent. https://www.sagaftra.org/contracts-industry-resources/member-resources/artificial-intelligence
- **Colour/loudness QC vocabulary to name in our dailies**: ITU-R **BT.2446-1** (HDR→SDR conversion methods), **BT.2100** PQ/HLG, **EBU R128 / ITU-R BS.1770** loudness — measurable with ffmpeg's `ebur128`, `volumedetect`, `astats`, `signalstats`, `ssim`, `psnr`, `libvmaf`, `blackframe`, `freezedetect` filters. https://ffmpeg.org/ffmpeg-filters.html · https://www.forasoft.com/learn/video-quality/articles-vqm/measuring-quality-ffmpeg-libvmaf

**How the trust UI should read (critic-proof framing)**
Show only what is verifiable and state the boundary in the same block: a per-shot card with `C2PA 2.5 manifest: signed / not signed`, `IPTC aiSystemUsed: ffmpeg`, `human review: this beat was refused because…`, `this seal proves a statement was made about these bytes at this time; it does not prove the film is true, good, or authorised` — which is the sentence our own Act IV fence already prints. That fence, rendered *next to* the standard names, is the differentiator.
