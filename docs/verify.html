What a receipt says — and nothing more

A receipt may make exactly four statements. Each one carries its own limit, printed with it. Anything outside these four is not a weak claim on a receipt — it is a claim a receipt cannot make at all.

1 · The bytes matched the commit hash

At seal time, this exact set of bytes hashed to the commit hash recorded on the receipt.

Limit: the match binds the receipt to those bytes and nothing else. Change one bit and the hash is a different hash — the receipt then describes a file you no longer hold.

2 · The seal existed on a date

A ledger entry for this seal existed on the date printed on the receipt.

Limit: existence is not truth. A false statement sealed on a date is still a false statement — the date proves a statement was made then, not that it was correct then.

3 · Which stages produced which hashes

Each production stage that ran is recorded with the artifact hash it handed on.

Limit: it records what the platform measured between stages. Inside a tool, the platform sees only the files it passed in and the files it received back.

4 · What was not checked

The receipt lists the checks the platform deliberately did not run on this job.

Limit: it is a policy disclosure, not a proof of absence. It tells you what was skipped; you decide what that costs the claim.

What no receipt can ever say

A seal is an audit trail. It is not digital truth. Plainly, a receipt cannot say:

That the content is true. A sealed sentence can be a sealed lie.

That the material is authentic or original. The seal never saw its way back to a camera.

That anyone authorised this. Consent, licence and rights are separate questions with separate records.

That two things coexisted or were made together. Matching receipts do not merge into one history.

That the film is good. There is no hash for craft.

That the record is permanent or tamper-proof. Seals age with the systems that hold them; none of them are ours to guarantee.

How it works: commit, seal, anchor, verify

Commit

The output files are hashed into one commit hash — a fixed-length fingerprint of exactly these bytes, in this arrangement.

Depends on: nothing external. It is arithmetic on a file.

Seal

The commit hash and the receipt's statements are written into the studio ledger, dated at entry.

Depends on: the ledger's own clock and retention. A seal is an audit trail entry, not a guarantee of duration.

Anchor

Optionally, the ledger's root is published to third-party canonical roots so a stranger can locate the entry without trusting our database.

Depends on: external systems we do not own — the roots a seal reaches belong to their operators, not to this platform. Anchoring is opt-in, on finished work only.

Verify

Anyone recomputes the hash of the bytes they hold and compares it to the receipt, then checks the seal entry exists. It costs nothing, needs no account, and that is not going to change.

Depends on: you having the bytes and an honest copy of the receipt.

The chain's honest shape: commit fixes the bytes, seal dates the statement, anchor (when opted in) makes the statement findable outside our walls, verify is a comparison anyone can do. No link in that chain makes a claim true.

The free door, stated

Checking a receipt: zero

Free forever, for anyone, with no account and no payment. Not a trial, not a tier — verification is priced at zero as a rule of the platform, because a seal that only its maker can check is worthless.

The one revenue channel

The platform charges for one thing: an anchor of finished work, published to third-party roots on request. Anchoring is an opt-in service; the receipt and its check are not.

Never charged, never gated

Reading a receipt, recomputing a hash, comparing the two — these stay outside the paywall permanently. If a check ever sits behind a price, that door on this page is the one to point at.
